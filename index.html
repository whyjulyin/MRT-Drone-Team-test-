1. Introduction

REPORT PAGE 06 / 33

1. Introduction

The University Rover Challenge (URC) is a robotics competition for university students, organized annually by the Mars Society near the Mars Desert Research Station (MDRS) in the desert of southern Utah, United States. The competition tasks student teams with designing and building next-generation Mars exploration rovers capable of executing field missions that simulate real planetary surface operations.

Field performance is evaluated across 4 distinct missions—Delivery, Autonomy, Equipment Servicing, and Astrobiology—each worth 100 points for a maximum achievable score of 400 mission points, though the drone can only be utilized for the Delivery and Autonomy missions. The Delivery Mission requires rovers to retrieve and deliver objects weighing up to 5 kg across challenging terrain over distances up to 1 km. The Autonomy Mission demands full autonomous navigation through waypoints as well as astronaut assistance and tool retrieval tasks without any human teleoperation. Overall team standings combine field mission points with engineering evaluations from the System Acceptance Review (SAR), which is submitted prior to the field competition.

1.1 Project Overview & Problem Statement

For this competition, our rover is expected to traverse undulating terrain at an estimated distance of up to 2 km. This introduces the risk of communication loss between the rover and our base station due to signal attenuation over long ranges and obstructed line-of-sight due to natural obstacles. As such, the aerial drone subsystem is deployed specifically during the Delivery Mission and Autonomy Mission to support ground operations. Serving as a Non-Line-of-Sight (NVLOS) RF relay operating at an elevated hover altitude of 60 m AGL, the drone acts as an airborne repeater bridging primary telemetry and video links between the Command and Control base station and the ground rover when line-of-sight is severed by canyon walls and terrain ridges. Additionally, the drone provides aerial reconnaissance by scouting ahead of the ground rover to identify optimal driving paths, locate scattered mission equipment, and detect elevated ArUco markers beyond the field of view of ground-level cameras.

Figure 1: Elevated drone communication relay bypassing terrain obstructions to maintain an uninterrupted 2.4 GHz link between the base station and rover.

A flat quadcopter layout with fixed-pitch propellers was selected over complex coaxial or hexacopter platforms. This design minimizes dry structural weight, eliminates mechanical singlepoint failure modes in fine desert dust, and delivers high hover aerodynamic efficiency under strict competition mass rules.

1.2 URC2027 Competition Missions & Restrictions

To simulate flight performance under lower atmospheric density, the drone must always carry a non-functional dummy mass equal to its battery weight. The total weight is capped at 5.0 kg, which includes both the dummy payload and a FAA Remote ID module around 20g. Operating altitude is restricted to a maximum of 120 m above ground level in compliance with FAA regulations.

Environmental conditions at the Mars Desert Research Station feature summer temperatures reaching up to 46 degrees Celsius based on historical climate data, placing severe thermal management demands on onboard electronics. Operational wind guidelines strongly recommend that the platform withstand sustained desert winds of 48 km/h, requiring a minimum top speed of 36 km/h when carrying the dummy payload. The entire competition spans a 1-mile square near the Mars Desert Research Centre.

1.5 Overall Design Objective

The principal objective of the drone subsystem is to deliver a robust aerial asset tailored specifically to URC mission constraints. The platform is engineered to achieve a sustained hover endurance of at least 20 minutes at 5.0 kg MTOW while carrying the 1:1 dummy mass. It targets an operational visual reconnaissance radius of 2 km and maintains uninterrupted RF signal relay coverage over a 1 km operating radius. By carrying an airborne repeater module, the drone bridges telemetry and video feeds between the ground rover and base station while transmitting HD video streams for real-time terrain mapping and elevated ArUco marker identification.

2. Literature Review

REPORT PAGE 10 / 33

2. Literature Review

The team's predecessor platform suffered from structural, electrical, and scaling bottlenecks. The baseline platform utilized a 580 mm wheelbase frame equipped with standard 4008 motors, 15inch folding propellers, and a 6S 10,000 mAh Li-Ion pack flying at a total weight of 2,654 g. Combined four-motor thrust testing revealed a 27.1% thrust deficit between isolated bench tests (1,900 g per motor) and full system integration (1,385 g per motor). Analytical decomposition confirmed that battery voltage sag under a 39.7A load accounted for 20.8% of this reduction, while turbulent inter-motor propeller wash interference on the compact frame accounted for the remaining 7.9%.

The redesigned platform addresses these empirical loss mechanisms by optimizing mass distribution, expanding frame geometry to an 800 mm carbon fiber wheelbase to eliminate rotor wash overlap, and utilizing short 10 AWG silicone power leads with anti-spark XT90-S interconnects to minimize internal resistance losses. Avionics integration was upgraded to Bidirectional DShot telemetry with dynamic PID notch filtering, suppressing mechanical frame resonance and reducing motor waste heat by around 15 degrees Celsius in ambient desert conditions.

3. Technical Design

3.1 Propulsion System

3.1.1 Design Requirements and Methodology

The quadcopter is designed for communication relay and terrain surveying, with a maximum take-off mass (MTOW) of 5 kg. Based on these requirements, preliminary propulsion sizing was conducted to estimate the required motor thrust and propeller diameter, followed by the selection and comparison of motor–propeller combinations alongside the airframe design. A mass budget was then developed using the estimated component masses to determine the allowable battery mass.

The design follows an iterative approach, integrating propulsion sizing, mass budgeting, battery selection, and mechanical integration. Changes in component or battery mass affect the total aircraft mass, required thrust, power consumption, and predicted endurance. Finally, a battery mass sensitivity analysis and endurance optimisation were performed to estimate flight time and select a suitable configuration while satisfying the 5 kg MTOW constraint.

3.1.2 Preliminary Propulsion Sizing

Hover and Maximum Thrust Requirement

At the maximum take-off mass (MTOW) of 5 kg, each of the four motors must provide 5,000/4 = 1,250 gf of hover thrust (approximately 12.26 N). A preliminary design thrust-to-weight ratio of 2.0 gives a target maximum available thrust of at least 2,500 gf per motor. This reserve supports climb and manoeuvring capability, although actual wind tolerance also depends on control authority and battery-limited thrust.

3.1.4 Battery Selection and Endurance Optimisation

Battery Mass Sensitivity Analysis

Following the preliminary propulsion-system selection, a battery mass sensitivity analysis was conducted to investigate the competing effects of battery mass on available energy and required propulsion power. Under the competition requirement, the dummy payload mass must equal the battery mass. Although a heavier battery provides greater stored energy, it also increases the hover thrust, and electrical power required to support the aircraft.

To study this trade off, a parametric sensitivity analysis was performed across candidate battery masses. For each case, the total aircraft mass (including equal dummy mass), required thrust, motor operating point, electrical power demand and predicted hover endurance were recalculated using manufacturer performance data. A constant specific energy of 287 Wh/kg was assumed, representative of the selected high-energy lithium-ion battery class (355.2 Wh / 1.24 kg = 286.5 Wh/kg). This enables a consistent comparison of the mass–energy trade-off without introducing differences between individual battery products.

As illustrated in Figure 2, predicted hover endurance increases across the feasible battery mass range, although the incremental benefit decreases as battery mass rises. This diminishing-return behaviour occurs because the gain in usable battery energy is increasingly offset by the additional propulsion power needed to lift the battery and dummy payload. Therefore, battery capacity was maximised within the 5 kg maximum take-off mass (MTOW) constraint. The selected battery mass is determined by this constraint and the availability of suitable commercial batteries, rather than by the unconstrained maximum of the endurance curve.

3.5 Technical Challenges

3.5.1 Piloting from within the Base Station

One major design challenge was ensuring operation of the drone from within our base station. Rather than having a clear line-of-sight from the operator to the drone, the base station is an enclosed area, which cuts off radio connection via antenna. As such, we are required to find extension cables to place the antenna outside the base station.

The competition guidelines recommend a length of 20 meters of antenna cable from the base station to the antenna housing area. This poses some additional difficulties, as long distance cables introduce additional cable loss into our link budget calculations. For our design we selected a 20 meter LMR400 coaxial cable as the extension cable from the controller to its antenna and a similar length HDMI cable to transmit the video feed from the goggles to the monitor. Firstly, the signal attenuation of the HDMI cable varies depending on the cable’s construction and specifications. 3.5.2 Estimated System Parameters

Our second major design challenge is having reliance on manufacturer datasheets for component specifications. Unlike other subsystems in our CCA, budget and time constraints limit our ability to develop and test multiple prototypes. Consequently, important parameters like component dimensions, mass, battery performance and motor efficiency are derived primarily from manufacturer data, where components are tested under controlled environments. These values may differ in actual operating conditions, which introduces uncertainty in our design.

This issue is further exacerbated by the interdependence of design parameters. One example is our battery mass, which determines the dummy payload’s mass and affects flight endurance. Hence, an underestimation of battery mass would significantly impact our flight time estimation. Such dependencies mean that small inaccuracies would have cumulative impact on our drone’s performance.

4.1 Mechanical Design and Integration

The symmetric four-arm frame is sized to accommodate 18-inch propellers. The central structure is divided into an upper electronics platform for the flight controller and communication hardware and a lower compartment for the battery and camera/gimbal. The battery is located near the geometric centre to reduce its contribution to centre-of-gravity offset; the full massweighted CG must still be calculated, including the equal dummy mass. The underslung camera supports downward terrain observation, while the landing gear provides ground clearance.

Preliminary CAD view of the component arrangement.

The initial CAD model was developed before the physical components were received. With the components now available, the next steps include 3D scanning components with complex geometries, refining mounting interfaces, verifying propeller clearance and wiring accessibility, determining the overall center of gravity (CG), and finalizing mechanical integration. Structural and flight-test validation have not yet been conducted at this interim stage.

4.2 Repeater Module Testing

Preliminary testing was conducted to evaluate compatibility between our selected GL-AR300Ext Module and the Rocket M2 radio, which are part of the 2.4GHz communications relay between the base station, drone and lander. The objective was to establish a wireless connection between the two devices and verify basic network connectivity through ping testing.

Testing & Results

Testing between different configurations of the GL.iNet module, we successfully connected the Wi-Fi repeater in both router and extender modes, through reconfiguration of IP addresses. Our final ping test after rounds of troubleshooting successfully transmitted four packets at a response time of 2 to 30 milliseconds. These results demonstrate achievable connection between the two devices under short range conditions.

Further testing is required to ensure long-range communication with realistic outdoor operating conditions, and end-to-end relay functionality must also be validated before we can confirm the suitability of this architecture.

7. Summary

To conclude, our project aims to develop a custom quadcopter for supporting the NUS Mars Rover Team in the University Rover Challenge 2027, with our main capabilities of aerial reconnaissance and establishing non-line-of-sight communications relay. Overall, we laid the foundation of our design in this semester through an iterative design process, and developed a clear plan for full integration and operational testing of the drone in semester 2.

Current Progress

Throughout this first semester, our team established our preliminary system architecture and component selections, conducting subsystem-level analysis to evaluate the feasibility of the proposed design. Significant progress was made in propulsion and battery optimisation, mechanical design, electrical system integration and wireless communication architecture. Using iterative mass budgeting, flight endurance estimation, and link budgeting calculations, we developed a design that both achieves mission objectives and stays within competition restrictions. Initial wireless repeater connectivity has also been successfully established in this term.

Future Work

Nevertheless, uncertainties remain in terms of actual flight performance, due to a heavy reliance on manufacturer specifications and theoretical calculation frameworks. While conservative design margins have been incorporated to account for our potential discrepancies, further experimental validation required to verify actual performance and reliability. In semester 2, we aim to complete mechanical and electrical integration of the parts, along with individual component testing, before proceeding with flight tests, CV integration and ultimately mission planning for the final competition. These activities will help to address our uncertainties in flight performance and evaluate the system against our initial design requirements, to ensure an operationally ready drone before our final submission and URC2027 Competition.
